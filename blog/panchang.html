the panchang is five numbers, not a mood

every auspicious timing u have ever been quoted was assembled from exactly five measurements of a single day. here is what each one is, how it is calculated, and why two panchangs can disagree about the same tuesday.

AUGUST 6, 2026 · 12 MIN READ

someone tells u a date is auspicious. u nod. u have no idea what was measured, and nobody offers.

here is what was measured. the panchang is five specific quantities computed for a given day and place, and every muhurat, festival date and auspicious window in Indian practice is assembled from those five and nothing else. the word literally means five limbs: pancha, five, and anga, limb. this piece takes each limb apart, shows how it is calculated, and gives u a calculator for any date u like.

the short version

  • panchang means five limbs. those five are tithi, vara, nakshatra, yoga and karana.
  • four of the five come from just two numbers: where the sun is and where the moon is.
  • tithi is the moon's angular distance ahead of the sun, cut into 12 degree steps. thirty per lunar month.
  • vara is the weekday, and the Vedic day runs sunrise to sunrise, not midnight to midnight.
  • nakshatra is the moon's position among the 27 lunar mansions. yoga is sun plus moon. karana is half a tithi.
  • two panchangs disagree when they use a different ayanamsa, a different location for sunrise, or a different rule for which tithi owns a day. all three are real and none is an error.

what a panchang actually is

A panchang is a table of five calculated quantities for each day: tithi, vara, nakshatra, yoga and karana. It is the working document of the Hindu lunisolar calendar, and every auspicious timing in Indian practice is assembled from those five values.

the panchang is often treated as a mystical almanac. it is closer to a tide table. someone computes where the sun and moon are, applies four definitions and a weekday, and prints the result.

the striking thing once u see the definitions is how little goes into it. four of the five limbs come from two numbers: the ecliptic longitude of the sun and the ecliptic longitude of the moon. tithi is their difference. yoga is their sum. nakshatra is the moon alone. karana is half the tithi. only vara, the weekday, comes from somewhere else.

that is worth holding onto when someone quotes a complicated-sounding auspicious window at u. underneath it there are two angles and a sunrise time.

limb one: tithi, the lunar day

A tithi is the time it takes for the moon to gain 12 degrees on the sun. Thirty tithis make one lunar month, split into two fortnights: shukla paksha as the moon waxes and krishna paksha as it wanes. Tithis vary in length from about 19 to 26 hours.

this is the limb that does the most work, and the one behind most festival dates.

take the moon's longitude, subtract the sun's, and normalise to a number between 0 and 360. divide by 12. the whole number u get is the tithi index, from 0 to 29.

  • indices 0 to 14 are shukla paksha, the bright fortnight, when the moon is waxing.
  • indices 15 to 29 are krishna paksha, the dark fortnight, when it is waning.
  • the 15th of shukla paksha is Purnima, the full moon. the 15th of krishna is Amavasya, the new moon.

the detail that gets missed: tithis are not equal in length. the moon's orbit is elliptical, so its speed relative to the sun varies, and a tithi runs anywhere from roughly 19 to 26 hours. that is why a tithi can start mid-afternoon, or skip a sunrise entirely, or cover two sunrises. the calendar consequences are real:

  • a kshaya tithi is one that begins and ends between two sunrises, so no day carries it. it is skipped.
  • an adhika tithi covers two sunrises, so it is counted twice.

this is exactly why Hindu festival dates move around the Gregorian calendar, which we covered in why Hindu festival dates change every year.

one more useful property: tithi is ayanamsa-independent. it is the difference between two longitudes, so shifting both by the same amount changes nothing. every panchang in the world agrees on the tithi, whatever ayanamsa it uses. the same is not true of nakshatra or yoga.

limb two: vara, the weekday that starts at dawn

Vara is the weekday, named for the seven visible grahas: Ravivara for the Sun, Somavara for the Moon, and so on. The important difference from the western weekday is that the Vedic day runs from sunrise to sunrise, so a birth at 3am belongs to the previous vara.

the correspondence to the English names is not a coincidence. Sunday, Monday and Saturday are transparent, and the other four run through Norse substitutions of the Roman gods: Tuesday from Tiw for Mars, Wednesday from Woden for Mercury, Thursday from Thor for Jupiter, Friday from Frigg for Venus. two traditions independently named the week after the same seven moving objects, because those seven are what u can see.

the sunrise-to-sunrise rule matters more than it sounds. someone born at 4am on a Wednesday is, in panchang terms, born on Tuesday. this affects which planet is treated as the day's ruler, which fasting day applies, and which vara appears in a birth record. it is a common source of confusion when comparing an Indian record with a hospital record.

limb three: nakshatra, where the moon slept

The nakshatra of a day is whichever of the 27 lunar mansions the moon is passing through. Each is 13 degrees 20 minutes wide, so the moon crosses roughly one per day. It is the limb most used for choosing a time to begin something.

the moon takes about 27.3 days to circle the zodiac, so ancient Indian sky-watchers marked the 27 places it stops. ur birth nakshatra is one of these. a day's nakshatra is the one the moon is passing through that day.

the calculation is simple: take the moon's sidereal longitude and divide by 13 degrees 20 minutes. the whole number is the nakshatra index. each splits again into four padas of 3 degrees 20 minutes.

unlike tithi, nakshatra is ayanamsa-dependent. it is measured against a fixed sidereal zero point, so a panchang using Lahiri and one using Raman can differ near a boundary. it is a small difference and it matters exactly when u are close to an edge.

nakshatra is the limb that does most of the work in muhurat, choosing a time to begin something. some nakshatras are traditionally favoured for particular activities: Pushya for almost anything, Rohini for planting and building, Ashwini for medicine and travel. the full list, with what each one is traditionally good for, is in 27 nakshatras, ranked by how messy they are.

limb four: yoga, the sun and moon added together

The yoga is calculated by adding the sun's longitude to the moon's and dividing the total into 27 divisions of 13 degrees 20 minutes. There are 27 named yogas, from Vishkambha to Vaidhriti. It is the limb people know least and it has nothing to do with the physical practice.

this is the limb that confuses everyone, partly because the word is doing double duty. panchang yoga is not the yoga of postures, and it is also not the yoga of chart combinations like gajakesari yoga. same Sanskrit word, three different technical meanings.

the calculation: add the sun's sidereal longitude to the moon's, normalise to 0 to 360, divide by 13 degrees 20 minutes. that gives an index from 0 to 26, and each index has a name. the sequence runs Vishkambha, Priti, Ayushman, Saubhagya, Shobhana, Atiganda, Sukarma, Dhriti, Shula, Ganda, Vriddhi, Dhruva, Vyaghata, Harshana, Vajra, Siddhi, Vyatipata, Variyana, Parigha, Shiva, Siddha, Sadhya, Shubha, Shukla, Brahma, Indra, Vaidhriti.

because it adds two moving longitudes rather than subtracting them, yoga advances faster than tithi and typically changes about once a day.

a handful are traditionally considered inauspicious, most commonly Vyatipata, Vaidhriti, Ganda, Atiganda, Shula and Vyaghata. those are the ones that get avoided when picking a muhurat. the rest are treated as neutral to favourable, which is worth knowing before anyone tells u the yoga on ur wedding date is a problem: most of them are not.

limb five: karana, half a tithi

A karana is half a tithi, or 6 degrees of moon-minus-sun separation. There are 60 in a lunar month but only eleven distinct names: seven movable ones that repeat eight times, and four fixed ones that appear once each around the new moon.

the least consequential limb, and the one most panchang readers skip. it is included for completeness and because it does appear in muhurat rules.

divide the moon-minus-sun separation by 6 instead of 12 and u get 60 karanas per lunar month. the naming is where it gets structurally odd:

  • the seven movable karanas, Bava, Balava, Kaulava, Taitila, Gara, Vanija and Vishti, repeat eight times through the cycle, filling 56 of the 60 slots.
  • the four fixed karanas, Kimstughna, Shakuni, Chatushpada and Naga, appear exactly once each, clustered around the new moon.

the one people mention is Vishti, also called Bhadra. it is traditionally avoided for beginning auspicious activity, and it comes round eight times a lunar month, which is roughly every third or fourth day. that frequency is worth knowing: an inauspicious marker that appears eight times a month is a scheduling preference, not a warning.

how muhurat gets built from the five

A muhurat is an auspicious window assembled by checking the five limbs against the activity being planned. A practitioner looks for a favourable nakshatra and tithi, avoids the inauspicious yogas and Vishti karana, and avoids the daily bad windows such as Rahu Kaal.

this is where the five limbs stop being trivia and start deciding when a wedding happens.

the standard process, simplified but not misrepresented:

  1. check the nakshatra. different activities have traditionally favoured nakshatras. this does most of the filtering.
  2. check the tithi. some tithis are avoided for particular activities. the 4th, 9th and 14th of each fortnight are broadly avoided for auspicious beginnings.
  3. check the vara. each weekday has activities it favours, tied to its ruling graha.
  4. avoid the inauspicious yogas, principally Vyatipata and Vaidhriti.
  5. avoid Vishti karana.
  6. avoid the daily inauspicious windows, above all Rahu Kaal, a roughly ninety minute period whose timing depends on the weekday and on local sunrise and sunset.

notice what happens as u stack those filters. six constraints against a finite number of hours leaves very few windows, which is precisely why auspicious wedding dates cluster and why halls in India are booked solid on a handful of days a year.

there is also Abhijit muhurat, roughly the 48 minutes around local solar noon, traditionally treated as auspicious for almost anything and useful when nothing else in the day is clean.

why two panchangs disagree about the same day

Three legitimate reasons. They may use different ayanamsa values, which shifts nakshatra and yoga near boundaries. They may compute sunrise for a different city, which shifts the vara and which tithi owns the day. And they may follow different regional rules for assigning a tithi to a date.

this happens constantly and it is almost never anyone making a mistake.

different ayanamsa

Lahiri is the Indian government standard, but Raman, Krishnamurti and others are in real use and differ by a degree or more. that never changes the tithi, which is a difference of longitudes, but it can change the nakshatra or yoga when the moon is near a boundary.

different location

the panchang is computed for a specific place, because sunrise defines when the day starts. sunrise in Kolkata and sunrise in Mumbai are nearly two hours apart. a tithi ending in that gap belongs to different days in the two cities. this is the most common source of disagreement and the least understood.

different regional convention

there is more than one rule for which tithi a date carries. the widespread convention is the tithi running at sunrise, but some traditions use the tithi at a different reference moment or apply their own rules for festivals specifically. the amanta calendar of western and southern India ends its month at the new moon, and the purnimanta calendar of the north ends it at the full moon, which shifts month names for a fortnight at a time.

the practical upshot: for anything that matters, use a panchang computed for ur town and following ur family's tradition. a national average is fine for reading and wrong for planning.

what gets repeated, and what is actually true

The panchang is a mystical document.

It is a computed table. Four of its five limbs come from two numbers, the sun's longitude and the moon's, and the fifth is the weekday. You can verify every entry against an ephemeris.

A panchang is the same everywhere in India.

It cannot be. The day begins at sunrise, and sunrise in Kolkata is nearly two hours before Mumbai. Tithis ending in that gap land on different dates in the two cities.

An inauspicious yoga on my date means I should move it.

Only a handful of the 27 yogas are traditionally avoided. The majority are neutral or favourable, and a yoga changes about once a day, so the label is far more common than it sounds.

Vishti karana is a serious warning.

It comes round eight times a lunar month, roughly every third or fourth day. Something that frequent is a scheduling preference, not a hazard.

Rahu Kaal is a fixed time of day.

It depends on the weekday and on local sunrise and sunset, so it moves through the year and differs by city. A fixed printed time is an approximation for one location.

mistakes worth not making

  • using a panchang computed for a different city. sunrise defines the day, so location is not a detail.
  • assuming ur weekday matches the vara. anything before local sunrise belongs to the previous vara, which surprises people born early in the morning.
  • confusing panchang yoga with chart yogas. same word, unrelated technique. gajakesari yoga has nothing to do with the daily yoga.
  • treating the tithi as a fixed length day. tithis run from about 19 to 26 hours, which is exactly why the calendar behaves the way it does.
  • picking a muhurat from a national app for a family event. regional convention and local sunrise both matter, and the family tradition usually decides.

frequently asked questions

what is a panchang?

a table of five calculated quantities for a given day: tithi, vara, nakshatra, yoga and karana. the word means five limbs. every auspicious timing in Indian practice is built from those five.

what are the five limbs of the panchang?

tithi, the lunar day. vara, the weekday. nakshatra, the moon's lunar mansion. yoga, the sun and moon longitudes added. karana, half a tithi.

what is a tithi?

the time the moon takes to gain 12 degrees on the sun. there are thirty per lunar month, fifteen waxing in shukla paksha and fifteen waning in krishna paksha. they run between about 19 and 26 hours.

why do tithis vary in length?

because the moon's orbit is elliptical, so its speed relative to the sun changes. that variation is why some tithis get skipped and others counted twice.

what is the difference between tithi and a normal day?

a normal day is a fixed 24 hours. a tithi is defined by an angle between the moon and sun and runs from about 19 to 26 hours, so the two drift against each other constantly.

what is vara?

the weekday, named for the seven visible grahas. the Vedic day runs sunrise to sunrise, so anything before local sunrise belongs to the previous vara.

what is yoga in the panchang?

the sun's longitude plus the moon's, divided into 27 named divisions. it is unrelated to the physical practice of yoga and unrelated to chart yogas such as gajakesari.

what is karana?

half a tithi, 6 degrees of separation. sixty per lunar month with eleven distinct names: seven movable ones repeating eight times and four fixed ones near the new moon.

what is Vishti or Bhadra karana?

one of the seven movable karanas, traditionally avoided for starting auspicious work. it comes round eight times a lunar month, so it is far more frequent than its reputation suggests.

what is Rahu Kaal?

a roughly ninety minute daily period traditionally avoided for beginning something. its timing depends on the weekday and on local sunrise and sunset, so it is not a fixed clock time.

what is Abhijit muhurat?

roughly the 48 minutes around local solar noon, traditionally treated as broadly auspicious. it is the usual fallback when nothing else in the day is clean.

why do two panchangs show different dates?

three legitimate reasons: a different ayanamsa, a different city used for sunrise, or a different regional rule for assigning a tithi to a date. none of these is an error.

what is the difference between amanta and purnimanta?

two ways of ending a lunar month. amanta, used in western and southern India, ends at the new moon. purnimanta, used in the north, ends at the full moon. the same day can carry different month names in the two systems.

do I need a panchang for my birth chart?

no. the chart is calculated from planetary positions directly. the panchang gives u the tithi, vara, nakshatra, yoga and karana of ur birth, which is traditional context rather than chart input.

can I calculate a panchang myself?

yes. the sun's and moon's longitudes plus a sunrise time give u all five limbs. the calculator on this page does exactly that in ur browser, and shows which ayanamsa it used.

the five things worth remembering

  1. five limbs: tithi, vara, nakshatra, yoga, karana. four of them come from just the sun's and moon's longitudes.
  2. tithi is 12 degrees of moon-minus-sun separation, runs 19 to 26 hours, and is ayanamsa-independent so every panchang agrees on it.
